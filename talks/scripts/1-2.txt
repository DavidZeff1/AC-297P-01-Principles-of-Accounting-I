Saying you want to be an accountant is a lot like saying you want to be a doctor. There are specialties, and they're pretty different from each other. Accountants work in three broad places: public accounting, private accounting, and government.

Let's start with public accounting. These are firms that serve the general public. A business, a nonprofit, or just a regular person hires an accounting firm the same way they'd hire a law firm. Public accountants do three main kinds of work.

First, auditing. An auditor examines the transactions and systems behind a company's financial reports, and then gives an independent opinion on whether those statements are presented fairly. [pause] Think of it like a home inspection before you buy a house. You don't just trust the seller; you want someone independent to look.

Second, tax. Preparing and filing returns, sure, but also advising clients BEFORE they make a decision, so they understand the tax consequences ahead of time.

Third, consulting. That can be almost anything, from designing an information system to studying how a company makes its products.

Public accounting usually requires a license. In the United States, each state licenses Certified Public Accountants, or CPAs. Other countries have similar titles, like Chartered Accountant. The license tells the public: this person met real standards for education, exams, and experience, and they've agreed to follow a code of ethics.

Next, private accounting, sometimes called industry accounting. These accountants work for one employer, a business big or small, or a not-for-profit like a hospital, university, or charity. They figure out what things cost, build budgets, analyze investments, handle taxes and finance. Some are internal auditors. And here's a distinction worth remembering. An internal auditor is an employee looking inward, checking their own company's controls, protecting its resources. An external auditor is an independent outsider, reporting to the public. Same word, "auditor," very different seat at the table.

Then there's government. Every level, local, state, and national, needs accountants. At the federal level you'll find them at the IRS, collecting taxes and examining returns. At the GAO, the watchdog that audits how federal money is spent. At the SEC, which oversees financial reporting by public companies. And even at the FBI, where forensic accountants follow the money in fraud cases.

[pause] Now, the most important part of today. Ethics. Investors and lenders make big decisions based on financial statements. If those statements aren't honest, the whole system falls apart. So the profession builds in several safeguards. There's a code of ethics every licensed accountant must follow. There's the independent audit. There's government oversight, like the SEC. And, [chuckles] there's the ever-vigilant lawyer, because people who are hurt by bad reporting can sue.

[warmly] So here's the takeaway. Accountants work in public firms, private companies, and government. CPAs are licensed to serve the public, auditors give independent opinions, and through all of it, integrity is the whole job. People trust accountants with their money.
