[warmly] Welcome back. Last time we learned the big equation: assets equal liabilities plus owners' equity. Today we're going to put it to work, and watch what happens when a business actually DOES something.

Every time a business does something with money, we call it a transaction. Buying, selling, borrowing, paying. And each transaction changes the numbers. But here's the beautiful part. If it's recorded properly, a transaction never breaks the equation. Ever.

There are really only two ways that happens. Either one asset goes up and another goes down by the same amount, so the totals don't move. Or both sides of the equation go up, or both go down, together.

Let's watch it with Edelweiss Corporation, the company from last time. They start with twenty-five thousand dollars of cash and fifty thousand of accounts receivable. That's money customers owe them.

Transaction A. A customer pays ten thousand dollars that they already owed. Cash goes up ten thousand. Accounts receivable goes down ten thousand. [pause] Notice, this is not a new sale. The company just traded one asset, an I-O-U, for another asset, cash. Total assets? Unchanged.

Transaction B. Edelweiss buys thirty thousand dollars of equipment and borrows the money to pay for it. Equipment goes up thirty thousand. Loans payable, a liability, also goes up thirty thousand. Both sides rise together, so the equation still balances.

Now, Transactions C and D are where students usually get tripped up, so let's slow down.

Transaction C. Edelweiss does five thousand dollars of work for a customer, who promises to pay later. Accounts receivable goes up five thousand. Easy. But what's the other side? [pause] Here's the logic. Doing work for customers is revenue. Revenue increases income. And income, when it's kept in the business, becomes retained earnings, which is part of equity. So retained earnings goes up five thousand. Assets up five thousand, equity up five thousand. Balanced.

Transaction D. Edelweiss pays three thousand dollars of expenses, maybe rent or utilities. Cash goes down three thousand. And expenses reduce income, which reduces retained earnings. So equity goes down three thousand too. Still balanced.

See the pattern? Revenue pushes retained earnings up. Expenses pull it down.

Let's define those carefully. Revenue is the benefit a business gets from providing goods or services to customers. Expenses are the costs of doing business, the outflows it takes to produce those goods and services.

[pause] And one more thing. In everyday conversation, people say "revenue" and "income" like they mean the same thing. In accounting, they don't. Revenue is the total amount generated by the business. It's the top line of the income statement. Some countries call it turnover. Income is what's left after you subtract all the expenses. That's the bottom line. So: revenues minus expenses equals income.

A coffee shop that sells a million dollars of coffee has a million in revenue. But if it spent nine hundred fifty thousand running the place, its income is only fifty thousand. Big difference.

[warmly] So, to recap. Every transaction changes at least two things, and the equation always stays in balance. Revenue raises retained earnings, expenses lower it. And revenue is the top line, income is the bottom line. You've got this. See you next lesson.
