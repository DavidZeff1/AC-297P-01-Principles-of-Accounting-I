[warmly] Today's question: how should a merchant's income statement actually be laid out? It sounds like a formatting detail, but the format can reveal, or hide, what's really going on.

There are two main styles. The single-step income statement is simple. List all the revenues and gains together, list all the expenses and losses together, and subtract once. Done.

The multiple-step income statement breaks the results into stages. It starts with net sales, subtracts cost of goods sold to get gross profit. Then it subtracts operating expenses, usually split into selling expenses and general and administrative expenses, to get income from operations. Next comes a section for other items, like interest, and gains or losses from things outside the main business. That gives income before taxes. Subtract income tax, and you arrive at net income.

[pause] So why go to all that trouble? Here's the reason. Imagine a company whose core business is actually struggling. But this year, it sold an old building at a big gain. On a single-step statement, that gain gets blended in with everything else, and net income might look perfectly healthy. On a multiple-step statement, you'd see weak income from operations, with a one-time gain propping up the bottom line. That's a really important thing for an investor to know. Unmasking effects like that is the strongest argument for the multiple-step format.

Now, let's talk about percentages, because dollars alone don't tell the whole story. Two ratios are especially useful.

Gross profit margin is gross profit divided by net sales. For Hunter Company, that's three hundred seventy thousand divided by six hundred fifty-three thousand, about fifty-six and two-thirds percent. In other words, after paying for the goods it sold, Hunter keeps about fifty-seven cents of every sales dollar.

Net profit on sales is net income divided by net sales. For Hunter, thirty-nine thousand divided by six hundred fifty-three thousand, about six percent. So, after all expenses, Hunter keeps about six cents of each sales dollar.

Smart managers and investors watch these percentages over time. Even a small change in business conditions can shift the relationship between revenues and costs, and the percentages catch it early.

Last, a quick global view. International standards don't require a detailed income statement format, so single-step versus multiple-step matters less there. Instead, companies classify expenses either by nature, meaning what the cost is, like salaries or advertising, or by function, meaning what it's for, like manufacturing, sales, or administration.

[warmly] So, the takeaways. Single-step is simple but can hide weak operations. Multiple-step shows gross profit, operating income, other items, taxes, and net income in stages. And gross profit margin and net profit on sales help you track performance over time. Nice work today.
