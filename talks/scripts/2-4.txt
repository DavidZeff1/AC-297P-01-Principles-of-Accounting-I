[warmly] So far we've analyzed transactions, written them in the journal, and posted them to the ledger. Today we do a quick self-check, called the trial balance.

A trial balance is simply a list of every account in the ledger, with its balance. Accounts with debit balances, like assets and expenses, go in the debit column. Accounts with credit balances, like liabilities, equity, and revenue, go in the credit column. Then you add up both columns. Since every journal entry had equal debits and credits, the two totals should match. For Xao, both columns come to sixty-one thousand three hundred dollars. [pause] Balanced.

Keep in mind, the trial balance isn't a financial statement. It's an internal check. If the totals don't match, you know there's an error somewhere, and you have to find it. Trust me, it's much easier to be careful as you go than to hunt for a mistake later.

But here's the catch. A balanced trial balance does NOT prove everything is right. It only proves debits equal credits. Plenty of mistakes keep them equal. If you forget to record a transaction entirely, both sides are missing the same amount, so it still balances. If you record the same transaction twice, both sides get doubled, still balanced. And if you post an amount to the wrong account, say Rent Expense instead of Utilities Expense, the totals don't change at all. So a balanced trial balance is a good sign, but it's not a guarantee.

The trial balance is also a handy springboard to the financial statements. Revenues and expenses go to the income statement. For Xao, revenue was eleven thousand and expenses were two thousand eight hundred, so net income was eight thousand two hundred. That flows into retained earnings, and the assets, liabilities, and equity go onto the balance sheet. In the next chapter, we'll add adjustments that make these statements fully up to date. For now, think: ledger, then trial balance, then statements.

Next, let's get organized. A chart of accounts lists every account the company uses, usually with a number. It's a lot like zip codes for mail. A common pattern: accounts starting with one are assets, two are liabilities, three are equity, four are revenues, and five are expenses. Many systems let you type the number instead of the name. And numbers can be split further. If Accounts Receivable is one-oh-two, each customer could get their own sub-number.

That leads to our last idea: control accounts and subsidiary accounts. Say customers owe a company nineteen thousand dollars in total, spread across three customers. The company needs the total, for the balance sheet, and it also needs to know what each customer owes, to send them bills. So it keeps a separate subsidiary account for each customer. The Accounts Receivable account in the general ledger is the control account, and its balance must always equal the total of all the customer accounts. Computers are great at keeping those in sync.

[warmly] So, to wrap up. The trial balance checks that debits equal credits, but it can't catch every error. Statements flow from it. A chart of accounts numbers everything. And a control account always equals the sum of its subsidiary accounts. Good work today.
